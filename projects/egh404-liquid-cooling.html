Where single-phase cooling runs out

A boundary-consistent comparison of single- and two-phase direct-to-chip liquid cooling for AI accelerator racks above 200 kW.

Kalan Ferguson, Queensland University of Technology, October 2026

Proposal stage. The literature review, method and evaluation plan are complete. The preliminary analysis runs on a synthetic dataset; no experiment has been carried out yet.

Validated today
52 kW per rack
Needed next
180 to 220 kW
Proposed test matrix
270 runs, 3 scales
Deliverable
A measured P* map

Introduction

Single-phase cooling is validated to 52 kW per rack. Next-generation racks need 180 to 220 kW.

Topic
Direct-to-chip liquid cooling of AI accelerators. A cold plate on each GPU carries heat away by warming a liquid (single-phase) or boiling a refrigerant (two-phase).
Problem
Single-phase is the only mode validated at rack scale (Heydari et al., 2024). Two-phase has been tested only at chip and channel scale, with incompatible metrics (Lee et al., 2026; Morse et al., 2021). The limit of single-phase cannot yet be quantified.
Questions
RQ1At what GPU power and coolant condition does single-phase cooling exceed its case-temperature limit, measured on the same boundary as two-phase? RQ2Does two-phase extend that limit at rack scale without dryout or flow maldistribution?

Literature review

No study compares both modes on one boundary above package scale.

Each study draws its boundary at a different scale and reports a different metric, and the verdict flips with the metric (Lee et al., 2026).

My literature review found the apparent superiority of two-phase cooling is largely an artefact of mismatched scales and metrics. On Lee et al.'s same-rig comparison, single-phase leads on thermal resistance by 8.7% while two-phase leads on energy efficiency by 78%. The better architecture depends on the framework applied, not only the physics.

Two-phase also carries a failure mode single-phase does not. Its best heat transfer sits right next to dryout, so operating near its optimum means accepting a margin to a discontinuity. Nobody has located that margin at chip-relevant heat flux, or tested two-phase flow distribution across a rack.

Motivation, rationale and objectives

Rack heat load now exceeds the validated evidence by 3.5 to 4.2 times.

Objectives

  1. One boundary and one metric set for both modes.
  2. Measure the single-phase limit P* across power, flow and inlet temperature.
  3. Test whether two-phase extends P* at rack-slice and full-rack scale.
Hypothesis

Two-phase holds Tcase below 90 °C beyond the single-phase P*, at equal or lower pumping power.

Methods

One control volume, measured at three scales.

  1. Package bench

    One thermal test vehicle. The single-phase cold plate and the two-phase evaporator swap on one mount, with a full-factorial sweep of power, flow and inlet temperature.

    Cooling mode becomes the only variable, as in Lee et al. (2026).

  2. Rack-slice

    Eight test vehicles on parallel cold plates sharing supply and return headers, up to 1.8 kW each (about 15 kW).

    Reproduces uneven branch flow, the rack-scale failure mode in Heydari et al. (2024).

  3. Full-rack validation

    One NVL72-class production rack (180 to 220 kW) at an industry partner, same sensors, live workloads.

    Tests the bench-derived P* map under real facility conditions.

I ruled out CFD alone because it cannot yet predict dryout onset at these geometries, and field data alone because it cannot isolate cooling mode.

Resources

Two coolant loops, 15 kW of test load, one partner rack, no human participants.

Evaluation methods

270 controlled runs, an 8‑branch rack-slice, then a live rack.

  1. CommissionHeat balance closes within ±5%
  2. Test matrix2 × 5 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 270 randomised runs
  3. Collect1 Hz; steady when dT/dt < 0.1 °C/min
  4. AnalyseFit case temperature against power and flow; P* with 95% confidence intervals
  5. Scale upRack-slice, then a live rack
Rack-slice acceptance
Every branch within ±10% of mean flow, and 100 hours held at 90% of predicted dryout.
Live rack
Predicted against measured case temperature under real workloads.
Hypothesis test
Supported if the two-phase P* interval lies above the single-phase interval at equal or lower pumping power: a paired comparison with a two-way ANOVA, mode × power.

Preliminary data analysis and reflection

On synthetic data, two-phase resistance sits well below single-phase until dryout, then jumps 43%.

Synthetic dataThese 240 runs come from a dataset I generated in Assessment 2 from textbook correlations, with 2 °C of random noise. They test the analysis pipeline, not the physics. Hover or focus a point for its run details.
Supports the direction
The analysis separates the two modes and resolves a dryout step, which is exactly what the experiment must measure.
Contradicts the literature
On real hardware, two-phase resistance was 8.7% higher (Lee et al., 2026). My generator assumed two-phase wins on resistance; measured hardware shows the opposite. That is the strongest argument for the experiment.
Limitations
Synthetic and circular; no pumping power; one GPU; dryout onset uncalibrated.

Outcomes and benefits

Deliverable: a measured map of where single-phase stops being sufficient.

Outcomes

  1. An open benchmark dataset: both modes on one boundary, at bench, rack-slice and rack scale.
  2. A mode-selection map: measured P*(V̇, Tin) for each mode, with 95% confidence bounds.
  3. Design limits: dryout margin and branch flow-split tolerance for parallel cold plates.
  4. A peer-reviewed paper with open analysis code.
Operators
Cooling architecture chosen on measured limits, not vendor claims.
Engineers
Validated margins for cold-plate and manifold design above 1 kW per GPU.
Energy and environment
Lower pumping energy, warmer-water heat reuse, PFAS-free fluids.

Questions I prepared for

Each answer states the trade-off openly.

Your synthetic data was generated from the relationships you then found. What is it worth?
It validates the analysis pipeline, not the physics. It showed the method separates the two modes, resolves a dryout step, and that a linear model is wrong for two-phase. It also exposed the contradiction with Lee et al. (2026), which is the strongest argument for the experiment.
Why not test a full 200 kW rack in the lab?
Cost and facility power. Heydari et al. (2024) show the rack-scale failure mode is flow distribution across parallel branches, which eight branches reproduce at real per-GPU heat flux. Stage 3 then validates on one production rack. Extrapolating from 8 to 72 branches is the main stated limitation.
Why not CFD?
Two-phase CFD cannot yet reliably predict dryout onset at these geometries. Experiment first; CFD is then useful for interpreting manifold flow distribution.
How will you detect dryout?
A step rise and growing fluctuation in wall and case temperature at constant power, cross-checked against exit vapour quality from the energy balance, with a sight glass at the evaporator outlet.
Which refrigerant?
A low-GWP dielectric screened against PFAS restrictions. Classic HFE fluids such as HFE-7100, used by Lee et al., are affected by 3M exiting PFAS manufacturing. Fluid choice is an ethical and supply-chain decision as well as a thermal one.

Conclusion

Single-phase is proven today. This project measures where it stops being enough.

Gap
No study compares single- and two-phase cooling on one boundary above package scale.
Method
One control volume at bench, rack-slice and full-rack scale: 270 controlled runs, endurance and field validation.
Contribution
The first boundary-consistent P* map for direct-to-chip cooling.
Impact
Evidence for cooling-architecture decisions as AI racks pass 200 kW, where thermal management already draws about 40% of data-centre electricity (Wu et al., 2025).
